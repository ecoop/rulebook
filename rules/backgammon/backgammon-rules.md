# Backgammon — Rules

Backgammon is a two-player board game combining strategy and dice. Each player
races their fifteen checkers around and off the board; the first to bear off all
of their checkers wins. Play is enlivened by hitting opponents' exposed checkers
and by the doubling cube, which raises the stakes.

## Equipment

- A board of 24 narrow triangles called **points**, grouped into four quadrants
  of six points each: each player's **home board** and **outer board**.
- Fifteen **checkers** per player, in two contrasting colors.
- Two six-sided **dice** (each player typically has their own pair) and a dice
  cup.
- A **doubling cube** marked 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, used to track the stakes.

The points are numbered for each player from 1 to 24, starting from that
player's home board. The two players move in opposite directions, each toward
their own home board (points 1 through 6).

## The Setup

Each player begins with their fifteen checkers arranged as follows, counting
from their own perspective:

- Two checkers on the 24-point.
- Five checkers on the 13-point.
- Three checkers on the 8-point.
- Five checkers on the 6-point.

The two players' starting positions mirror each other.

## Object of the Game

Each player tries to move all fifteen of their checkers into their own home
board and then **bear them off** (remove them from the board). The first player
to bear off all fifteen checkers wins the game.

## Movement of the Checkers

Players alternate turns. To start the game each player rolls a single die, and
the higher number moves first, playing the two dice just rolled; thereafter each
turn begins by rolling both dice. The two numbers rolled indicate how many
**pips** (points) the player may move their checkers:

- The numbers on the dice may be applied to the same checker or to two different
  checkers. For example, a roll of 5 and 3 lets you move one checker 5 pips and
  another 3 pips, or move one checker 8 pips — provided the intermediate point
  (either the 5 or the 3 away) is open.
- A checker may move only to an **open point**: one not occupied by two or more
  opposing checkers.
- **Doubles** are played twice over: a roll of two 4s, for instance, allows four
  moves of 4 pips each.
- A player must use both numbers of a roll if legally possible (all four numbers
  for doubles). If only one number can be played, the player must play that one;
  if either but not both can be played, the higher must be played. If no legal
  move is possible, the turn is forfeited.

## Hitting and Entering

A point occupied by a single checker is called a **blot**. If an opposing
checker lands on a blot, the blot is **hit** and placed on the **bar** (the
ridge dividing the board).

A player with one or more checkers on the bar must **enter** them into the
opponent's home board before making any other move. To enter a checker, the
player uses one die value to place it on the corresponding point in the
opponent's home board, provided that point is open. If neither die yields an open
entry point, the player forfeits the turn. Once all checkers are off the bar, any
remaining dice numbers may be played normally.

## Bearing Off

Once a player has moved all fifteen of their checkers into their own home board,
they may begin **bearing off**. A player bears off a checker by rolling a number
that corresponds to the point on which the checker sits (a 6 bears off a checker
from the 6-point, and so on).

- If there is no checker on the point indicated by a die, the player must make a
  legal move of a checker on a higher-numbered point.
- If there are no checkers on higher points, the player may (and must) bear off a
  checker from the highest occupied point using that die.
- Bearing off is not compulsory when a legal move within the home board is
  possible for that die, but players almost always bear off when they can.

If a checker is hit during the bear-off, its owner must bring it around the board
and back into the home board before bearing off may resume.

## The Doubling Cube

The doubling cube lets a player who feels they are ahead offer to double the
stakes. Before rolling on their turn, a player may propose a double. The opponent
may:

- **Take** (accept): play continues for double the previous stakes, and the
  opponent now owns the cube, gaining the exclusive right to make the next
  double.
- **Drop** (refuse): the game ends immediately and the refusing player loses the
  current stake (the value before the proposed double).

There is no limit to the number of times the cube may be turned during a game,
but each successive double may be offered only by the player who last accepted.

## Scoring: Gammons and Backgammons

The value of a completed game is the current value of the doubling cube
multiplied by the type of win:

- **Single game (1 point):** the loser has borne off at least one checker.
- **Gammon (2 points):** the loser has not borne off any checkers.
- **Backgammon (3 points):** the loser has not borne off any checkers *and* still
  has one or more checkers on the bar or in the winner's home board.

The result is multiplied by the doubling-cube value; for example, a gammon with
the cube on 4 is worth 8 points.

## Optional Rules

- **Crawford rule:** in match play, when either player first reaches a score one
  point short of the match, the doubling cube is not used for the next single
  game (the "Crawford game"). Doubling resumes afterward.
- **Jacoby rule:** in money play, gammons and backgammons count only as a single
  game unless the doubling cube has already been turned. This discourages playing
  on for a gammon when the cube is still in the middle.
- **Automatic doubles:** if both players roll the same number on the opening
  single die, the cube may be turned to 2 and the dice re-rolled (usually limited
  by agreement).
- **Beavers:** a player who is doubled may immediately redouble ("beaver") while
  retaining ownership of the cube.
