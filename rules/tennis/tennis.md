# Tennis — sample rules

A racket sport for two players (singles) or two teams of two (doubles), who hit a
ball back and forth over a net. These rules describe ordinary singles play for
teaching and retrieval demos, not a tournament's official rulebook.

1.1 The court. Tennis is played on a flat rectangular court 78 feet long, divided
across the middle by a net. A singles court is 27 feet wide; doubles adds a strip
called the alley along each side, making the court 36 feet wide. The net is 3 feet
high at the centre and a little higher at the posts.

1.2 The lines. The lines at each end of the court are the baselines, and the lines
along the sides are the sidelines. Between the net and each baseline, a service
line runs across the court, and a centre service line joins the two service lines
down the middle. Together they mark out four service boxes, two on each side of the
net. A ball that touches any part of a line counts as landing inside the court.

1.3 Racket and ball. Each player hits the ball with a strung racket. The ball is a
hollow rubber ball covered in felt, about 2.6 inches across. A player may hold the
racket in either hand, and may change hands during play.

2.1 Object of the game. Players try to hit the ball over the net so that it lands in
the opponent's court and cannot be returned. Each rally ends with one player
winning a point; points build into games, games into sets, and the player who wins
the required number of sets wins the match (see section 5).

2.2 Choosing ends and service. Before play starts, the players toss a coin or spin a
racket. The winner chooses whether to serve or receive first, or which end of the
court to start from, and the other player takes the remaining choice.

2.3 Returning the ball. After the serve, players take turns hitting the ball over
the net. A player must hit the ball before it bounces twice on their side. They may
hit it after one bounce, or before it bounces at all (a volley), except that the
return of serve must be allowed to bounce first.

3.1 The serve. Each point begins with a serve. The server stands behind the
baseline, between the centre mark and the sideline, throws the ball up by hand and
hits it with the racket before it touches the ground. The serve must clear the net
and land in the service box diagonally opposite.

3.2 Serving from alternate sides. In each game the first point is served from the
right half of the court, and the server switches sides after every point. The same
player serves for a whole game; service passes to the opponent at the start of the
next game.

3.3 Faults and double faults. A serve is a fault if it misses the correct service
box, hits the net and falls back, or misses the ball entirely, or if the server
steps on or over the baseline before hitting it (a foot fault). After one fault the
server tries again. Two faults in a row, a double fault, lose the point.

3.4 Lets. A serve that touches the net and then lands in the correct service box is
a let: it does not count, and that serve is taken again. A point is also replayed
as a let when play is interrupted by something outside the players' control, such
as a ball rolling onto the court from next door.

4.1 Losing a point. A player loses the point if the ball bounces twice on their
side before they hit it; if they hit it into the net or outside the opponent's
court; if they hit it before it has crossed the net; or if the ball, while in play,
touches them or anything they are wearing or carrying other than the racket.

4.2 Touching the net. A player who touches the net, the net posts or the
opponent's court with their body or racket while the ball is in play loses the
point. During a rally, a ball that clips the top of the net and still lands in the
opponent's court stays in play; only on a serve is a net touch a let.

5.1 Scoring a game. Points in a game are called by name rather than counted: no
points is "love", then 15, 30 and 40. Winning a point at 40 wins the game, unless
both players have reached 40. The server's score is always called first.

5.2 Deuce and advantage. When both players reach 40 the score is called deuce. The
next player to win a point has the advantage; if they win the following point too,
they win the game, and if they lose it the score returns to deuce. A game can only
be won from deuce by winning two points in a row.

5.3 Winning a set. A set is won by the first player to win six games, with a margin
of at least two games. At five games all, play continues until one player leads by
two, at 7-5, or the score reaches six games all.

5.4 The tiebreak. At six games all, a tiebreak decides the set. Points are counted
1, 2, 3 instead of 15, 30, 40, and the first player to reach seven points with a
lead of two wins the tiebreak and the set, recorded as 7-6. One player serves the
first point; after that, each player serves two points in turn.

5.5 Winning the match. A match is usually played as the best of three sets: the
first player to win two sets wins. Some major men's events play the best of five
sets, which takes three sets to win.

5.6 Changing ends. Players change ends of the court after the first, third and every
following odd-numbered game of each set, so that neither player keeps the better
side of the sun or wind. In a tiebreak they change ends after every six points.
